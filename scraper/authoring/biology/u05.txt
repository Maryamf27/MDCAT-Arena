# Biology Unit 5 Coordination and Control
@ 5.1
E|What is the term for a receptor that converts a stimulus into a nerve impulse?|Transducer|Effector|Amplifier|Neurotransmitter|Receptors transduce stimulus energy such as light or pressure into electrical signals.
E|Rods and cones of the retina are receptors for which stimulus?|Light|Sound|Pressure|Chemicals|Photoreceptors respond to light.
E|Taste buds and olfactory cells respond to which type of stimulus?|Chemical substances|Heat|Pressure|Light|They are chemoreceptors.
M|Hair cells of the organ of Corti are examples of which type of receptors?|Mechanoreceptors|Thermoreceptors|Photoreceptors|Chemoreceptors|They convert vibration of sound waves into nerve impulses.
M|Which receptors detect changes in temperature?|Thermoreceptors|Nociceptors|Baroreceptors|Photoreceptors|Thermoreceptors in skin and hypothalamus detect heat and cold.
M|Which receptors respond to damaging stimuli and produce the sensation of pain?|Nociceptors|Proprioceptors|Osmoreceptors|Photoreceptors|Nociceptors are free nerve endings that respond to tissue damage.
M|Muscle spindles and tendon organs that provide information about body position are called|Proprioceptors|Chemoreceptors|Photoreceptors|Thermoreceptors|Proprioceptors monitor stretch and tension of muscles and joints.
H|A sensory receptor converts stimulus energy into a graded change in membrane potential. What is this change called?|Generator potential|Action potential|Resting potential|Synaptic delay|If the generator potential reaches threshold, action potentials are triggered in the sensory neuron.
H|Which receptor responds to pressure and vibration in the deep skin?|Pacinian corpuscle|Meissner's corpuscle only|Rod cell|Taste bud|Pacinian corpuscles are encapsulated mechanoreceptors sensitive to deep pressure and vibration.
H|Why do you stop noticing the smell of a perfume after a few minutes in a room?|Receptors adapt and reduce firing to a constant stimulus|The chemical is destroyed in the air|The olfactory nerve is cut|Neurons stop producing neurotransmitter permanently|Sensory adaptation lowers the response to a steady stimulus so that changes can be detected.
@ 5.2
E|Which part of a neuron receives incoming signals from other neurons?|Dendrites|Axon terminal|Myelin sheath|Node of Ranvier|Dendrites are short branches that receive stimuli and carry impulses toward the cell body.
E|Which part of the neuron carries impulses away from the cell body?|Axon|Dendrite|Nucleus|Nissl body|The axon conducts impulses away from the cell body toward other cells.
E|Where is the nucleus of a neuron located?|Cell body (soma)|Axon|Dendrite|Myelin sheath|The soma contains the nucleus and most organelles.
M|Which cells form the myelin sheath around peripheral axons?|Schwann cells|Oligodendrocytes|Astrocytes|Microglia|Schwann cells wrap peripheral axons; oligodendrocytes form myelin in the CNS.
M|What are the gaps in the myelin sheath called?|Nodes of Ranvier|Synaptic clefts|Axon hillocks|Dendritic spines|Impulses jump from node to node in saltatory conduction.
M|What are Nissl bodies?|Clusters of rough ER and ribosomes in the cell body|Bundles of microtubules in axons|Vesicles in axon terminals|Gaps in the myelin sheath|Nissl bodies synthesise proteins needed by the neuron.
M|What do vesicles in the axon terminals contain?|Neurotransmitter|DNA|Myelin|Glycogen only|Vesicles release neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft.
H|Which region of the neuron is the site of initiation of the action potential?|Axon hillock|Dendrite tip|Myelin sheath|Synaptic knob|The axon hillock has a high density of voltage-gated sodium channels and acts as the trigger zone.
H|Loss of myelin in multiple sclerosis causes|Slowed or blocked nerve conduction|Faster conduction|Increased neurotransmitter synthesis|Loss of cell nuclei|Demyelination disrupts saltatory conduction.
H|Sensory neurons with cell bodies in dorsal root ganglia are best described as|Unipolar (pseudounipolar)|Multipolar|Anaxonic|Apolar|A single process leaves the cell body and divides into peripheral and central branches.
@ 5.3
E|What is a nerve impulse?|A wave of depolarisation (action potential) travelling along a neuron|A flow of blood through a nerve|A chemical released by endocrine glands|A movement of ions in the blood|A nerve impulse is an electrical signal conducted along the plasma membrane of a neuron.
E|What is the approximate resting membrane potential of a typical neuron?|-70 mV|+70 mV|0 mV|-10 mV|The inside of the membrane is negative relative to the outside at rest.
E|Which ions rush into the axon during depolarisation?|Sodium ions|Potassium ions|Chloride ions only|Calcium ions only|Voltage-gated sodium channels open and Na+ enters the axon.
M|Which ion movement is chiefly responsible for repolarisation?|Potassium ions leaving the axon|Sodium ions entering the axon|Chloride ions entering the axon|Calcium ions leaving the axon|K+ channels open and K+ moves out, restoring negative potential.
M|What ion movement does the sodium-potassium pump perform?|Three Na+ out and two K+ in per ATP|Two Na+ out and three K+ in per ATP|Three Na+ in and two K+ out per ATP|One Na+ out and one K+ in per ATP|The pump maintains the resting potential by pumping ions against concentration gradients.
M|What does the all-or-none law say about nerve impulses?|A stimulus at or above threshold gives a full-size action potential; below threshold gives none|Stronger stimuli produce larger action potentials|Weaker stimuli produce longer action potentials|Action potentials vary in size with distance|The amplitude of an action potential is constant regardless of stimulus strength.
M|Which factor increases the speed of conduction of nerve impulses?|Larger axon diameter and myelination|Lower body temperature|Shorter axon length|Absence of ion channels|Thick and myelinated axons conduct faster.
H|At approximately what membrane potential is the threshold for firing an action potential in a typical neuron?|-55 mV|-70 mV|+30 mV|0 mV|When depolarisation reaches about -55 mV, voltage-gated sodium channels open in large numbers.
H|Why does an action potential normally travel in one direction along an axon?|The refractory period of the region behind prevents it from firing again|Sodium channels exist only on one side|The axon is longer on one end|The neurotransmitter flows backward|The segment just fired is temporarily inactive, so the impulse moves forward.
H|Why does the membrane potential briefly drop below resting level after repolarisation?|Potassium channels close slowly, causing hyperpolarisation|Sodium channels remain open|The Na+/K+ pump is blocked|Chloride enters the axon|Voltage-gated K+ channels stay open a little longer than needed.
@ 5.4
E|What is a reflex action?|A rapid involuntary response to a stimulus|A slow voluntary movement|A hormonal response|A learned skill|Reflexes happen automatically without conscious thought.
E|The knee-jerk reflex is an example of which type of reflex?|Spinal reflex|Cranial reflex|Conditioned reflex|Learned reflex|The reflex centre for the knee jerk lies in the spinal cord.
E|A dog that salivates on hearing a bell after training shows which type of reflex?|Conditioned reflex|Unconditioned reflex|Monosynaptic reflex|Spinal reflex|Pavlov's dogs learned to associate the bell with food.
M|Blinking when an object comes rapidly toward the eye is a|Unconditioned reflex|Conditioned reflex|Learned skill|Voluntary action|It is inborn and needs no previous learning.
M|The pupillary light reflex is classified as a|Cranial reflex|Spinal reflex|Conditioned reflex|Voluntary reflex|The reflex centre lies in the brain, with cranial nerves carrying the signals.
M|The knee-jerk reflex differs from the withdrawal reflex in that it is|Monosynaptic|Polysynaptic|Conditioned|Cranial|The knee jerk has a single synapse between sensory and motor neurons.
M|A reflex that adjusts pupil size or heart rate involves which division of the nervous system?|Autonomic|Somatic only|Voluntary|Cerebral cortex only|Autonomic reflexes regulate involuntary functions of smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands.
H|Which reflex is polysynaptic and uses interneurons?|Withdrawal of a hand from a hot object|Knee jerk|Ankle stretch reflex only|Biceps tendon stretch reflex only|Pain withdrawal reflexes involve interneurons between sensory and motor neurons.
H|Which feature distinguishes a conditioned reflex from an unconditioned reflex?|It is acquired through experience and involves the cerebral cortex|It is present from birth|It never involves the brain|It involves only the spinal cord|Conditioned reflexes are learned and modifiable.
H|A rise in blood pressure slows the heart rate through baroreceptors. This is an example of an|Autonomic reflex|Conditioned reflex|Voluntary action|Spinal knee reflex|Baroreceptor reflexes function through autonomic centres in the medulla.
@ 5.5
E|Which is the correct sequence of components in a reflex arc?|Receptor, sensory neuron, CNS, motor neuron, effector|Effector, motor neuron, CNS, sensory neuron, receptor|Receptor, motor neuron, CNS, sensory neuron, effector|Sensory neuron, receptor, effector, CNS, motor neuron|The impulse travels from receptor to effector through the CNS.
E|What is the effector in a reflex arc?|A muscle or gland|A sensory organ|An interneuron|A spinal nerve root|Effectors carry out the response.
E|In which direction does a sensory neuron carry impulses?|Toward the central nervous system|Away from the central nervous system|Between muscle cells|From effector to receptor|Sensory (afferent) neurons conduct impulses to the CNS.
M|Which root of a spinal nerve carries sensory fibres?|Dorsal root|Ventral root|Both roots carry motor fibres only|Neither root|Sensory fibres enter the cord through the dorsal root and motor fibres leave via the ventral root.
M|Where are the cell bodies of sensory neurons of spinal nerves located?|Dorsal root ganglion|Ventral horn|Brain stem|Ventral root|The dorsal root ganglion lies just outside the spinal cord.
M|What is the role of an interneuron in a reflex arc?|Relays impulses from sensory to motor neurons within the CNS|Carries the response to the muscle|Detects the stimulus|Secretes hormones|Interneurons lie within grey matter and connect sensory and motor neurons.
M|Which receptor initiates the knee-jerk reflex?|Muscle spindle|Rod cell|Taste bud|Hair cell|Stretching of the quadriceps tendon stimulates muscle spindles.
H|If the ventral root of a spinal nerve is cut, which result is expected?|Loss of motor response while sensation is retained|Loss of sensation while motor function is retained|Loss of both sensation and movement|No change in the reflex|The ventral root contains motor fibres; the dorsal root remains intact for sensation.
H|In the spinal cord, where do the cell bodies of the motor neurons of a spinal reflex lie?|Ventral horn of the grey matter|Dorsal root ganglion|White matter tracts|Dorsal horn of the grey matter|Motor neuron cell bodies are in the ventral (anterior) horn, whereas sensory cell bodies lie in the dorsal root ganglion.
H|Why does a person become aware of pain only after withdrawing the hand from a hot object?|The reflex acts through the spinal cord while sensory signals also travel to the brain|The brain controls the reflex directly|Pain receptors act slowly|The effector sends the signal to the brain|The spinal reflex is faster; the impulse also ascends to the brain, giving conscious pain afterward.
