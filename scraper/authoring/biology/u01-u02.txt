# Biology Unit 1 Acellular Life, Unit 2 Bioenergetics. Format: D|question|correct|d1|d2|d3|explanation
@ 1.1
E|Which group of viruses infects bacterial cells?|Bacteriophages|Viroids|Prions|Mycoplasmas|Bacteriophages ("bacteria eaters") are viruses whose hosts are bacteria; viroids and prions are not viruses and mycoplasmas are bacteria.
E|Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) is a plant virus with which type of genome?|Single-stranded RNA|Double-stranded DNA|Double-stranded RNA|Single-stranded DNA|TMV is a helical virus whose genome is a single strand of RNA enclosed in a protein capsid.
E|What name is given to the protein coat that surrounds the nucleic acid of a virus?|Capsid|Plasmid|Peptidoglycan wall|Nucleoid|The capsid is made of protein subunits called capsomeres and protects the viral genome.
M|Which virus shows complex symmetry, having a polyhedral head joined to a helical tail?|T4 bacteriophage|Tobacco mosaic virus|Adenovirus|Influenza virus|T4 phage has an icosahedral head and a helical tail with tail fibres; TMV is purely helical and adenovirus is purely icosahedral.
M|Which virus–genome pairing is correct?|Poliovirus – single-stranded RNA|Herpes simplex virus – single-stranded RNA|Hepatitis B virus – double-stranded RNA|Influenza virus – double-stranded DNA|Poliovirus has a positive-sense ssRNA genome; herpes viruses carry dsDNA, hepatitis B has a partly double-stranded DNA genome and influenza has segmented ssRNA.
M|A virus particle has a lipid membrane with glycoprotein spikes outside its nucleocapsid. How is such a virus described?|Enveloped virus|Naked virus|Viroid|Prophage|The envelope is acquired from the host membrane as the virus buds out, and distinguishes enveloped from naked viruses.
M|Bacteriophage, tobacco mosaic virus and HIV are placed in separate categories mainly on the basis of which criterion?|The type of host they infect|The size of their ribosomes|Their mode of photosynthesis|Their ability to form spores|Hosts (bacteria, plants and humans respectively) are one of the classification criteria for viruses.
H|A virus has a single-stranded RNA genome and carries reverse transcriptase in its virion. To which group does it belong?|Retroviruses|Bacteriophages with dsDNA|Naked dsDNA viruses|Viroids|Retroviruses copy RNA into DNA using reverse transcriptase before integrating into host DNA; HIV is an example.
H|Which list contains only diseases caused by viruses?|Measles, mumps and rabies|Typhoid, cholera and tetanus|Malaria, amoebiasis and sleeping sickness|Ringworm, athlete's foot and candidiasis|Measles, mumps and rabies are viral; the second list is bacterial, the third protozoal and the fourth fungal.
H|Why are viruses not placed in any of the five kingdoms of living organisms?|They are acellular and can reproduce only inside host cells|They contain both DNA and RNA|They lack nucleic acids|They have ribosomes but no cell membrane|Viruses lack cellular organization and metabolic machinery and are obligate intracellular parasites; each virion contains either DNA or RNA, never both.
@ 1.2
E|Which agent causes AIDS?|Human immunodeficiency virus|Hepatitis B virus|Rhinovirus|Herpes zoster virus|AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome) is the late stage of infection by HIV.
E|Which cells does HIV primarily infect and destroy?|CD4+ helper T lymphocytes|Red blood cells|Neurons|Platelets|HIV binds the CD4 receptor, so helper T cells are its main targets, weakening the immune response.
E|Which activity can transmit HIV from an infected to an uninfected person?|Sharing contaminated syringes|A casual handshake|Sharing a drinking glass|Coughing and sneezing|HIV spreads through blood, sexual fluids and breast milk, not through casual contact.
M|Which enzyme enables HIV to make a DNA copy of its RNA genome inside the host cell?|Reverse transcriptase|Restriction endonuclease|DNA ligase|Helicase|Reverse transcriptase synthesizes DNA from the viral RNA template, which integrates into the host chromosome.
M|Why do people with advanced AIDS often die of infections that rarely harm healthy people?|Loss of helper T cells cripples the immune response, allowing opportunistic infections|HIV directly digests the tissues of the lungs|The patients lose all their red blood cells|HIV converts harmless bacteria into toxins|Opportunistic pathogens take advantage of the profound immune deficiency caused by helper T-cell depletion.
M|Which combination of features is typical of advanced HIV infection?|Persistent swollen lymph nodes, weight loss and recurrent opportunistic infections|High fever with a rash that lasts three days only|Sudden paralysis with loss of vision|Yellowing of skin with dark urine only|Chronic lymphadenopathy, wasting and repeated opportunistic infections characterize progression to AIDS.
M|By which route can an HIV-positive mother pass the virus to her baby?|Across the placenta, during delivery or through breast milk|Only through shared clothing|Only through air during conversation|Only through the baby's blood group antigens|Mother-to-child (vertical) transmission can occur in pregnancy, at birth or while breastfeeding.
H|Which illness is considered an opportunistic infection that is often linked with AIDS?|Pneumocystis pneumonia|Common cold|Seasonal allergic rhinitis|Simple acne|Pneumocystis jirovecii causes severe pneumonia mostly in people with markedly low CD4 counts.
H|A person infected with HIV a fortnight ago tests negative for HIV antibodies. What is the most likely explanation?|The test was done in the window period before antibodies were produced|HIV never stimulates antibody formation|The virus was cleared completely|The test detects only bacterial proteins|Antibodies take weeks to appear after infection, so an early antibody test can be falsely negative.
H|Which property of HIV makes it difficult to develop an effective vaccine?|Rapid mutation of its genome, producing antigenic variation|Its large double-stranded DNA genome|Its inability to enter any cell|Its lack of surface proteins|Reverse transcriptase lacks proofreading, so many variants arise and the virus escapes the immune response.

E|Into which small molecules are dietary proteins digested before they can be used in respiration?|Amino acids|Fatty acids|Monosaccharides|Nucleotides|Proteins are hydrolysed to amino acids, some of which are oxidised for energy.
E|What are the products of hydrolysis of a triglyceride?|Glycerol and fatty acids|Glucose and fructose|Amino acids and ammonia|Nucleotides and phosphate|Lipases split a triglyceride into one glycerol and three fatty acids.
E|Which two-carbon unit is produced when fatty acids are broken down by beta-oxidation?|Acetyl CoA|Pyruvate|Oxaloacetate|Lactate|Beta-oxidation successively removes two-carbon fragments as acetyl CoA.
M|What happens to an amino acid before its carbon skeleton can enter the respiratory pathway?|Its amino group is removed (deamination)|It is converted into glucose in the mitochondria|Its carboxyl group is joined to glycerol|It is polymerised into a protein|Deamination removes the amino group as ammonia, which the liver converts to urea; the remaining keto acid enters respiration.
M|At which point does glycerol from fat breakdown enter the respiratory pathway?|As a triose phosphate in glycolysis|Directly as acetyl CoA in the Krebs cycle|At the electron transport chain|At the end of fermentation|Glycerol is phosphorylated and converted to a triose phosphate (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate), an intermediate of glycolysis.
M|Compared with an equal mass of carbohydrate, complete oxidation of fat yields|More energy, because fats are richer in reduced C-H bonds|Less energy, because fats contain more oxygen|The same energy, since both give acetyl CoA|No energy unless converted to glucose first|Fats are more reduced than carbohydrates, so they release about twice as much energy per gram.
M|Where does beta-oxidation of fatty acids mainly take place in eukaryotic cells?|Mitochondrial matrix|Golgi apparatus|Nucleus|Lysosome|Fatty acids are activated in the cytoplasm and oxidised in the mitochondrial matrix, where the Krebs cycle also operates.
H|The respiratory quotient (RQ) is the ratio of CO2 released to O2 consumed. What RQ is expected when only carbohydrate is oxidised?|1.0|0.7|0.5|1.5|Oxidation of glucose releases 6 CO2 for 6 O2 consumed; fats give about 0.7 because they contain little oxygen.
H|How many acetyl CoA molecules are formed from complete beta-oxidation of one 16-carbon fatty acid such as palmitic acid?|8|16|4|2|Each acetyl CoA contains two carbons, so a C16 chain gives eight of them after seven rounds of beta-oxidation.
H|Which molecule is the common entry point to the Krebs cycle for carbon skeletons from glucose, fatty acids and many amino acids?|Acetyl CoA|Lactic acid|Ethanol|Urea|Acetyl CoA is the convergence point where carbohydrate, lipid and protein catabolism merge before entering the Krebs cycle.
